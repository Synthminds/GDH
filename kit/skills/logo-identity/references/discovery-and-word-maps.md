# Discovery, the competitor sheet, the type of mark and word maps

Read this file at steps 1 to 4 of `logo-identity`. It covers the discovery questions, the logo lines of the brief, the competitor sheet, the decision on the type of mark, the word map method and a worked example.

Evidence. The brief contents and the competitor audit come from the GDH research on logo method, which rests on secondary sources and recalled books. "A short, distinctive name often needs only a wordmark" comes from Print Magazine, confirmed by search only. "Differ from the category's norms" comes from Designing for Emotion, chapter 2. The word map method is studio practice written from memory. Treat every count in this file as a default.

Terms. A mark is the symbol. A wordmark is the name in drawn letters. A seed is one concrete visual thing that a concept can start from. The competitor sheet is one page with the marks of competitors, kept as reference.

## 1. Discovery questions

Ask only what the brief does not answer. Ask in small groups. Offer a proposed answer when the user is unsure, and mark it `(proposed)`.

The name:

1. What is the exact name, with its spelling and capitals? Is it final?
2. Is there a short form that people will say or type?
3. Is there a descriptor or a tagline that must sit with the logo?
4. How is the name pronounced? What does it mean, and in which languages?

The organization:

5. What does it do, in one sentence a customer would use?
6. Who must recognize the mark, and where do they first meet it?
7. What must people feel on first sight? Give three words, each with its "not".
8. What is true of this organization and of no competitor?

The field:

9. Who are the five closest competitors? Who is admired outside the field?
10. Which visual habits of the field do you want to avoid?
11. Is there a mark, a color or a word you already own and must keep?

The uses:

12. Where will the mark appear in the first year? Name the smallest and the largest use.
13. Which uses are hard: one-color print, embroidery, engraving, a tiny app icon, a dark vehicle?
14. Which scripts and markets must the wordmark work in?

The decision:

15. Who decides? Who else must agree?
16. What would make you reject a concept at once?
17. May generated images be used for ideas? What is the budget for paid generation?
18. Which countries and which classes of goods or services matter for a trademark search?

## 2. The logo lines of the brief

Write these lines in `design/brief.md`, or confirm them there. Each is one sentence.

| Line | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Name and its form | "Ebbmark", one word, capital E only. No tagline in the lockup. The name is fictional. |
| What it does | Shows today's tide and ferry times for one harbour. |
| Recognition moment | A phone home screen among weather apps, and a sign at the quay. |
| Three words | steady, not stiff; coastal, not nautical-themed; exact, not cold |
| Smallest and largest use | A 16px browser tab icon. A two-metre sign on a harbour wall. |
| Hard uses | One-color stencil on a wall; embroidery on a jacket |
| Must avoid | Anchors, waves and compass roses |
| Must keep | Nothing |
| Decider | The harbour manager |

A line the user did not state stays `(proposed)` until confirmed. Do not draw before the name is final. A name change discards the wordmark.

## 3. The competitor sheet

1. Collect the marks of five to ten competitors and two admired outsiders.
2. Take each mark from the organization's own site or press page. A connected `logo-search` tool can look up existing marks.
3. Record the source URL of each. These are references. They never enter a deliverable.
4. GATE: do not upload the sheet to an outside tool without a yes.
5. Put all marks on one page, in black, at one height. Then again at 32px.
6. Note the habits of the field:

| Look at | Write |
| --- | --- |
| Type of mark | How many are wordmarks, symbols, emblems |
| Shapes | The shapes that repeat: circles, shields, waves, arrows, initials |
| Letterforms | Sans or serif, case, weight, width |
| Color | The hues that repeat |
| Ideas | The pictures that repeat: the product, a globe, a leaf |

7. Write the avoid list from the sheet: three shapes, two colors and two ideas the field has used up.
8. Write the open ground: what no competitor does.

The sheet has a second job. At the end, the new mark goes on it. A reviewer must find it at once and confuse it with none.

## 4. Decide the type of mark

| Type | Choose when | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wordmark | The name is short and distinctive; the brand is new and needs name recognition | Needs a fallback for tiny square uses |
| Lettermark or monogram | The name is long, or an initial is already in use | Initials are common; hard to own |
| Symbol with wordmark | The mark must work alone in small square places, across languages | Two things to learn; more cost to build recognition |
| Emblem | A seal or badge suits the subject: a club, a craft, an institution | Detail fails at small sizes; needs a simplified version |

Decision rules:

1. Start from the uses. An app icon and a favicon need a square form. A wordmark alone does not give one.
2. A new, unknown brand gains most from showing its name. Default: a wordmark, with a simple square form derived from it.
3. A symbol earns its place when it carries the idea better than the letters can.
4. A name of twelve characters or more needs a stacked form or a monogram for narrow places.
5. A wordmark that must work in a second script needs that script drawn too, not typed.
6. ASK when two types fit. Show the tradeoff in one line each.

## 5. Word maps

A word map turns words into seeds. A seed is a concrete thing you can draw.

1. Write the name in the center of one map. Write each brand word in the center of its own map.
2. Around the name, write its letters, its sounds, its meaning, its parts and its shape. Note repeated letters, symmetry, ascenders and descenders.
3. Around each brand word, write objects, actions, materials, places and tools from the subject's world. Not synonyms.
4. Go two rings out. From "tide": gauge, mark on a wall, moon, ebb line, wet and dry.
5. Stop at forty words or more across the maps.
6. Cross out what the competitor sheet has used up.
7. Connect pairs across maps. A letter with an object. An object with an action. Two objects that share a shape.
8. Write each good pair as a seed: one line that names what you would draw.

Seed patterns that produce concepts:

| Pattern | Question | Example seed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Letter and object | Does a letter of the name share a shape with a subject object? | The three bars of the E as three tide levels |
| Two meanings in one shape | Can one shape read as two things? | A tide gauge that is also a ruler of hours |
| Negative space | Can the space between forms carry the second idea? | The gap between two letters as a channel between quays |
| Action | Can the mark show what happens, not what the thing is? | A line that rises and falls once |
| Part for whole | Which small part stands for the subject? | The notch of a gauge, not the sea |
| System | Can a simple rule generate the mark? | A row of bars whose heights are today's tide |
| Pure wordmark | Which single change makes these letters this name? | One letter cut at the water line |

Rules:

1. Default: twenty seeds or more before you draw.
2. A seed that pictures what the organization sells is the weakest kind. Keep it only when nothing else in the field does it.
3. A seed must survive in black at 16px. Cross out any seed that needs color, shading or fine detail.
4. Keep the maps. They go in the direction folder as evidence of the search.

## 6. Failure patterns

| Name | Sign | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No name lock | Drawing starts while the name is still open | Stop. Confirm the name. |
| Synonym map | The word map holds adjectives only | Add objects, actions and materials |
| Literal picture | A fish for a fish shop | Find the part, the action or the letter link |
| Field echo | The seed list repeats the competitor sheet | Cross out the used ideas first |
| Type by habit | A symbol added because logos have symbols | Decide the type from the uses |
| Missing uses | The hard uses were never asked | Ask for the smallest, the largest and the one-color use |
| Reference as asset | A competitor mark pasted into a deliverable | References stay on the sheet |

## 7. Worked example

Ebbmark, a fictional tide table app. Three words: steady, coastal, exact. Avoid: anchors, waves, compass roses.

- Competitor sheet: nine marks. Six use a wave. Four use navy with a cyan accent. Seven are symbols with a sans wordmark.
- Open ground: nobody shows the tide as a measured level. Nobody uses a wordmark alone.
- Type: wordmark, with a square form derived from the letter E for the app icon.
- Word map highlights: gauge, notch, high-water mark, table of hours, quay wall, stencil paint.
- Seeds kept: the three bars of the E as tide levels; the two b letters as gauge posts; a notch cut in each letter at one level; stencil letters as painted on a quay.
- Seeds crossed out: a wave under the name (used up); a gradient sea (needs color).
